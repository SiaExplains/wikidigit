Kalshi, the New York prediction market exchange, said on Thursday, October 29, that it raised $1 billion at a $40 billion post-money valuation, in a Series G co-led by Wellington Management and Sequoia Capital. One day earlier, Utah Attorney General Derek Brown filed criminal charges against the company and its two co-founders, CEO Tarek Mansour and COO Luana Lopes Lara: 13 felony counts and one misdemeanor, accusing Kalshi of illegally promoting online gambling. Within about 24 hours, investors priced the company 82% higher than they had in May, and a state prosecutor put its founders at risk of prison.

## A $40 Billion Round, Five Months After $22 Billion

[Kalshi's announcement](https://news.kalshi.com/p/kalshi-series-g-40-billion-valuation) lists Baillie Gifford, Stripes, Andreessen Horowitz, Coatue, Layer Global, Morgan Stanley Investment Management, Sequoia Global Equities, CapitalG, Hudson River Trading and Balyasny Asset Management as other investors. Reuters, citing a person familiar with the deal, reported that Tiger Global and Dragoneer also took part.

This is Kalshi's second $1 billion round in about five months. The May round valued it at $22 billion. [CNBC reports](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/29/kalshi-40-billion-valuation-funding-round.html) that Kalshi was worth $5 billion in October 2025, which makes the new price eight times what it was a year ago. CNBC credits the jump since May to the FIFA World Cup, the NFL season and betting on the midterm elections, along with fast growth in combo contracts, which work a lot like sportsbook parlays.

The company's own numbers, none of them audited:

- More than $1 trillion in annualized trading volume and more than $1 billion in annualized non-sports revenue
- More than 30 million accounts and over 75% of prediction market trading volume
- $83 billion of volume and more than 500,000 traders in perpetual futures, which launched only a few months ago
- About 500 institutional entities active in September; excluding market makers, they made up roughly 25% of volume

The revenue figure covers only non-sports markets, and the announcement doesn't say how much Kalshi earns from sports, which is what the legal fights are about. According to Covers, its busiest World Cup day was July 5, with $608 million in single-day volume, and it set a record on October 24 with $5.1 billion in contracts traded.

Earlier this month [Ramp went from $44 billion to $60 billion in four months](/article/ramp-60-billion-valuation-brex-capital-one), which looked fast at the time. Kalshi nearly doubled in five.

## What Utah Charged

[KUER reports](https://www.kuer.org/politics-government/2026-10-28/kalshi-prediction-market-utah-gambling-criminal-charges) that the charges include one second-degree felony count of pattern of unlawful criminal activity, 12 counts of online gambling promotion and one Class A misdemeanor of general gambling promotion. Mansour and Lopes Lara could face prison time if convicted. [The Block reports](https://www.theblock.co/news/business/2026-10-29-utah-criminal-charges-kalshi-432104) that the state accuses Kalshi of letting an undercover agent place wagers.

"A federal court has already ruled that Utah's gambling laws apply to Kalshi," Brown said in a statement. "Kalshi kept taking bets from Utahns anyway. That is illegal, and we're putting a stop to it." The federal ruling he means came on August 4, when a judge found that Kalshi isn't immune to Utah's gambling ban and denied its request for an injunction while it appeals.

Kalshi called the charges "a crazy overreach; Utah is clearly playing politics," and added: "We look forward to putting a stop to this in court, just like we did in Arizona." Arizona isn't settled, though. The state filed criminal charges earlier this year, and a federal judge blocked that case, but KUER reports that Arizona is now trying to revive it after a 9th Circuit ruling that went Nevada's way.

## Washington Is on Kalshi's Side, and the Courts Are Split

Utah's charges land in the middle of a turf war. On October 20, Brown filed a civil suit against Kalshi and two Susquehanna trading firms. On October 27, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission sued Utah, arguing that the state is intruding on federal jurisdiction. "Congress entrusted the CFTC to be the federal regulator with sole responsibility to oversee these markets," CFTC Chair Michael Selig said. The Block counts at least 10 states the CFTC has sued so far.

The courts haven't settled the question. CNBC notes that two federal appeals courts ruled in August and September that states can regulate sports event contracts, a third sided with the federal position, and the dispute will likely go to the Supreme Court. The answer decides whether states can treat Kalshi's sports contracts as gambling.

Kalshi isn't the only fintech whose plan rests on a federal regulator's say-so. [Ripple's new national trust bank charter](/article/ripple-national-trust-bank-occ-charter-icba-lawsuit) came while community banks were suing the OCC over whether it can grant those charters at all.

## Kalshi Wants to Be More Than a Betting Site

Kalshi says the new money will go into products outside prediction markets. It plans to build indices with S&P, launch single-stock perpetual futures and markets on the price of compute, grow its institutional sales team and expand in Latin America and Europe. Reuters reported that it wants to compete more directly with CME Group and Intercontinental Exchange.

An IPO could follow. In June, Mansour told CNBC's "Squawk Box" that Kalshi was considering going public, but not until 2027. "A company of our financial profile with the rate of growth that we're seeing, that sort of conversation has to happen," he said. The nearer test is the November 3 midterm vote, which Mansour has called "the first prediction market election."
