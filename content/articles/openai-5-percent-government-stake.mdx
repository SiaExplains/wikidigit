It also presented President Trump's comments as a reaction to this specific pitch, when the reported remarks were earlier comments about the general idea, and described OpenAI's April policy paper and Sen. Bernie Sanders' proposal less precisely than the sources do. Those passages have been corrected.*

OpenAI has spent the past year warning, in its own policy papers, that AI could displace workers faster than existing safety nets can handle. This week, [according to the Financial Times](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/02/openai-proposed-donating-5-of-its-equity-to-a-us-sovereign-wealth-fund/), Sam Altman offered a fix: give the American public a 5% stake in OpenAI itself, [worth roughly $42.6 billion](https://www.swisherpost.com/technology/openai-government-stake-altman-alaska-fund/) at the company's $852 billion valuation. Altman reportedly [discussed the idea with President Trump, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick](https://time.com/article/2026/07/03/openai-invest-ai-trump-administration-sam-altman/), framing it as a way to let ordinary citizens share in the upside of a technology that OpenAI itself keeps warning could hollow out their jobs.

The mechanism is borrowed from a 50-year-old idea. Alaska created its Permanent Fund in 1976 and has paid residents an annual dividend from its oil wealth [since 1982](https://pfd.alaska.gov/Division-Info/historical-timeline), and OpenAI wants to build something similar for AI: a Public Wealth Fund that would hold equity stakes handed over by AI companies and pass returns on to Americans. Critically, OpenAI isn't proposing to go it alone. The pitch reportedly envisions Anthropic, Google, and Meta contributing matching 5% stakes, though it is unclear whether any would agree, turning a single company's PR maneuver into a proposed industry-wide tax paid in equity rather than dollars. Notably absent from the ask are the chipmakers, Nvidia and AMD, who have arguably captured more of the AI boom's value than any single model developer.

The timing is not an accident. OpenAI has spent 2026 fending off a steady drumbeat of criticism over its valuation, its governance structure, and its trillion-dollar IPO ambitions, while simultaneously publishing [an April policy paper](https://the-decoder.com/less-work-equal-pay-openai-lays-out-its-vision-for-a-world-reshaped-by-superintelligence/) that called for four-day work week pilots at full pay, higher taxes on top capital gains and on corporate returns from AI-driven growth, and taxes on automated labor. A sovereign-wealth-style stake handed to the government does double duty: it's a hedge against the political backlash of being the company that automates away millions of white-collar jobs, and it's a preemptive answer to lawmakers who are already asking harder questions. Senator Bernie Sanders, for one, introduced a far more aggressive version of the same idea in June — a bill imposing a one-time 50% tax on the stock of "systemically important" AI companies, with the shares deposited into a public wealth fund.

Trump has sounded cautiously receptive to the general concept: he has previously said "there's something very interesting about it" and talked about arrangements where "the American public essentially becomes a partner with the companies." The White House did not comment on OpenAI's proposal itself. That's a notably different posture than the deregulatory, hands-off stance the administration has taken on AI policy elsewhere, and it hints at how much political pressure has built up around a handful of AI labs commanding valuations that rival entire national economies. Any real version of this proposal would almost certainly require an act of Congress, and getting Anthropic, Google, and Meta to voluntarily hand over billions in equity is a very different proposition than OpenAI floating the idea in a private meeting.

Strip away the populist framing and the proposal looks less like generosity and more like insurance. A government that owns 5% of OpenAI has a direct financial stake in the company succeeding, which makes aggressive regulation, antitrust action, or a forced breakup considerably less appealing. It also gives OpenAI a talking point every time critics point out that its technology displaces workers or its valuation dwarfs the market caps of companies that actually turn a profit. Whether the other AI giants play along, or whether this dies in a congressional committee, the fact that a 5% government stake is even being discussed at OpenAI's scale says something about how fused AI policy and AI ownership have become in 2026.
