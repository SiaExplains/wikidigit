Nearby dwarf galaxies have lost most of the oxygen their own stars made, according to a study built on NASA Hubble Space Telescope data and published in The Astrophysical Journal. Across 37 galaxies, the researchers found that between 4% and 25% of the oxygen is still there, about 11% on average, and nobody can yet say where the rest ended up. NASA announced the result on November 2. The paper, "GLOW. II. A Census of Oxygen in Low-mass Galaxies," is led by Rutgers astronomer Kristen Wingfield McQuinn, and it finds the galaxies are leaking oxygen faster than current computer models predict.

## How You Count Oxygen That Isn't There

Measuring what's missing means first working out what should be there. The galaxies in the Galaxies Losing Oxygen via Winds (GLOW) project were picked because they are close enough for Hubble to resolve their individual stars, which most known dwarf galaxies are not. According to Sci.News, they range from about 3 million to 3 billion times the mass of the Sun.

From the brightness and colors of those stars, the team rebuilt each galaxy's star formation history: when it made stars, how many, and how its chemistry changed over time. That gives an estimate of how much oxygen the stars produced over the galaxy's life. The team then counted what is left in stars, gas and dust, using data from the retired Spitzer Space Telescope, the Very Large Array and the Australia Telescope Compact Array. Most of the oxygen was gone, and nearly all of what remains sits in gas rather than in stars or dust, Sci.News reports.

"We know that the oxygen had to be inside of the galaxies at some point in the past, so the oxygen must have been ejected," said Grace Telford, an assistant professor at the University of Utah and a co-investigator on the project.

## Why Astronomers Track Oxygen in Dwarf Galaxies

Oxygen is the most abundant element after hydrogen and helium. Its production in stars is well understood, and it gives off light at specific wavelengths that ground telescopes can pick up. That makes it a clean tracer for how the elements stars forge spread through a galaxy and beyond it.

"So, it's really for practical reasons that we've chosen oxygen," McQuinn said. She is GLOW's principal investigator and also mission head of the Science Operations Center for NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope at the Space Telescope Science Institute.

A 2014 study of star-forming galaxies similar to the Milky Way found they had also lost a large share of their oxygen, and some of it turned up in the circumgalactic medium, the halo of gas that surrounds a galaxy. GLOW extends that census down to much smaller systems, whose masses fall in the range where simulations predict oxygen loss through supernovae should increase.

## Supernova Winds Beat the Simulations

The researchers blame galactic winds. Massive stars and supernova explosions pump energy into a galaxy's gas, and in a small galaxy that gas can be driven out entirely. "You need energy from massive stars and supernova explosions to drive material out of the host galaxies," Telford said.

Simulations already expected heavy losses, but the observations show even less oxygen left than the models predict. Per Sci.News, the authors compared their numbers with three cosmological simulations. All three reproduce the known relationship between a galaxy's mass and its metal content, and all three still have dwarf galaxies holding on to more metals than the observations show.

A simpler galaxy-evolution model matched the data better under three conditions: outflows remove proportionally more mass from smaller galaxies, the gas they blow out is about twice as metal-rich as the gas left behind, and the inflow of fresh gas onto these galaxies is suppressed by about 40%.

Location also seems to matter. Galaxies more than 3.3 million light-years from a massive neighbor kept less than 15% of their oxygen. Those in denser surroundings showed more scatter, and they include most of the highest retention values.

## The Missing Oxygen Still Has No Address

One option is that the oxygen is still close by, in each galaxy's circumgalactic halo. Earlier ultraviolet observations of highly ionized oxygen account for only about 1% to 7% of what was produced. After correcting for oxygen in other ionization states, the authors estimate the halos could hold 5% to 35%. Even at the high end, that leaves a large share that may have traveled into intergalactic space. McQuinn said tracking the oxygen also shows where, and how much, galaxies are enriching the space between them, so finding it is part of the project's goal.

GLOW's answer came from counting stars one at a time and checking the totals against simulations. ## What Comes Next for GLOW

McQuinn has an approved program to use the Roman Space Telescope to find fainter, smaller galaxies across a wide area of sky, then study their stars in detail with Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope to reconstruct how they formed. Telford will use Hubble to look at the individual massive stars that produce and eject oxygen in some nearby GLOW galaxies, to see how those stars behave in small galaxies. NASA and the National Science Foundation fund the project.
