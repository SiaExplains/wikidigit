The FCC on Wednesday, November 25, approved SpaceX's request to put up to 1 million satellites into orbit for Starmind, a network of data centers in space. The agency called it the first authorization in US history for a constellation of orbiting data centers. Amazon, one of three groups that petitioned to block it, had told the FCC in March that Starmind was a "lofty ambition rather than a real plan" and that SpaceX would need centuries to build it, CNBC reported. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr announced the decision on X, writing that the FCC "approved SpaceX's request for 1 million satellites." Elon Musk replied: "Much appreciated."

## What the FCC Actually Approved

Starmind is an optically linked constellation flying at altitudes between 500 and 1,002 kilometers (310 to 623 miles), according to GeekWire and Via Satellite. The satellites talk to each other with lasers. They use Ka-band radio only for limited backup and emergency operations under Starlink's existing authorization, and they connect with Starlink satellites, CBS News reported, citing a February FCC filing.

Via Satellite says each satellite carries a compute payload drawing 175 kilowatts on average and up to 250 kilowatts. Heat goes out through deployable liquid radiators, and the solar arrays are based on Starlink's. GeekWire reports that SpaceX said in August the compute payload would use Nvidia Rubin GPUs and Vera CPUs, and that the compute satellites would be built at its facility in Bastrop, Texas.

Via Satellite lists the license deadlines: one satellite in orbit by November 25, 2033, 10% of the constellation by 2035, 50% by 2038 and the rest by November 25, 2040. GeekWire adds that the FCC requires communications backups, regular updates and a detailed orbital debris mitigation plan.

"By moving compute capacity beyond Earth, SpaceX and the many competitors working on their own orbital data centers are meeting the moment," Carr said in the FCC's release, quoted by GeekWire.

## Amazon's "Centuries" Argument

Amazon, Viasat and the American Astronomical Society each filed a petition to deny. Amazon's March filing argued that SpaceX would take centuries to put a million satellites up, according to CNBC. Carr hit back that Amazon "should focus on the fact" that it is behind on its own deployment milestone. Amazon declined to comment after the approval.

SpaceX says it will move much faster than the FCC requires. President and COO Gwynne Shotwell told investors in October that SpaceX plans to start launching Starmind satellites in 2027, Via Satellite reported. SpaceX's IPO filing says it expects to begin deploying orbital AI compute satellites as early as 2028, according to CBS News. The company already rents out ground-based GPU capacity as [the AI industry's compute landlord](/article/spacex-colossus-ai-compute-landlord), and Starmind would extend that business into orbit.

## Astronomers Lost on the Night Sky

The American Astronomical Society said it was "disheartened and dismayed." It argued that putting data centers in low Earth orbit would undermine decades of federal investment in astronomical facilities and the public's enjoyment of the night sky. CNBC quoted astrophysicist and author Adam Becker: "Any serious attempt at this is going to further pollute the night sky."

The FCC set most of that aside. CNBC reports that the agency dismissed comments on subjects it has no authority over, including environmental concerns and optical astronomy. Via Satellite says the FCC decided no environmental review under NEPA was required, called concerns about satellites burning up in the atmosphere "speculative and not supported in the record," and said it does not limit the number of satellites that can be authorized with similar orbital parameters. Jan Hasselman, a senior attorney at Earthjustice, criticized the lack of NEPA review and the decision's timing during a holiday week, CNBC reported.

The Secure World Foundation, which filed comments instead of a petition to deny, asked for greater oversight and described constellations like this as a "qualitative shift," according to Via Satellite.

## Starmind Opens the Door for Everyone Else

Other companies are waiting on their own applications. Blue Origin has applied for up to 51,600 satellites under Project Sunrise, and Starcloud has applied for 88,000. GeekWire lists more: Cowboy Space, formerly Aetherflux, at 20,000 satellites, Sophia Space with a 10-satellite constellation, and Google's Project Suncatcher, whose first satellites are set to fly on New Glenn no earlier than 2029.

Analysts quoted by Via Satellite say orbital data centers "remain technically and economically unproven." When Texas voters elected a governor who [wants new data centers halted](/article/musk-224-million-texas-data-center-halt), Musk posted that data centers in space were looking "pretty appealing." Now his company has the license, and until 2033 to get the first satellite up.
