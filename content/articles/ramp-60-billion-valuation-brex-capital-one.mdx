Ramp, the New York corporate card and spend-management startup, raised about $1.85 billion at a roughly $60 billion valuation, [Bloomberg reported on Friday, October 9](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/ramp-hits-60-billion-valuation-203610650.html), citing people familiar with the matter. Dragoneer Investment Group and Thrive Capital led the round, Founders Fund wrote a large check, and the $60 billion figure is pre-money, so it doesn't include the new cash. That is $16 billion of added value in four months, up from the $44 billion Ramp was valued at in June. The twist sits right next to it: Brex, the startup that spent years as Ramp's closest rival, agreed in January to sell itself to Capital One for $5.15 billion. Two companies, one market, and a valuation gap of more than 11 to 1.

Ramp declined to comment to Bloomberg, and the three investors didn't respond, so treat the exact numbers as reported rather than announced. Bloomberg did first report in September that Ramp was in early talks at a $60 billion valuation, and Friday's story says those talks turned into a closed round.

## $44 Billion in June, $60 Billion in October

The speed is the story. In June, Ramp raised $750 million at a $44 billion valuation, a round [PYMNTS describes as a Series F](https://www.pymnts.com/news/investment-tracker/fintech-investments/2026/ramp-valued-at-60-billion-following-new-funding-round/). At the time, the company said it worked with 70,000 businesses, up from 50,000 at the start of 2026, and Bloomberg reported that its annualized revenue had passed $1.5 billion.

Run the rough math and the new round prices Ramp at about 40 times that June revenue figure. That's a growth multiple, not a software-company-at-rest multiple, and investors are paying it because the growth has been unusually steep for a fintech. PYMNTS reports that Ramp's total payment volume grew about 170% year over year in March 2026, its fastest rate in three years, and that the company says it is 20 times the size it was three years ago.

CEO Eric Glyman has framed the opportunity as bigger than cards. "Finance is going through the biggest structural change since the spreadsheet," he has said, adding that "we still only serve a fraction of the market."

## How Brex Went From Peer to Price Tag

Until this year, Bloomberg counted Brex as Ramp's closest startup competitor. Brex hit a [$12.3 billion valuation in January 2022](https://www.crowdfundinsider.com/2026/01/257812-brex-acquired-by-capital-one-for-5-15-billion/). Four years later, [Capital One agreed to buy it for $5.15 billion](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/capital-one-acquire-fintech-company-012739989.html) in a stock-and-cash deal, less than half that peak. Brex said more than 35,000 companies use its platform, and the deal was expected to close in mid-2026.

Capital One CEO Richard Fairbank said the purchase "accelerates this journey, especially in the business payments marketplace." Brex founder Pedro Franceschi stays on to run it inside the bank.

Put the two numbers side by side and the market's verdict is blunt. Bloomberg now calls Ramp "by far the highest valued startup focused on corporate spending management." A category that once looked like a two-horse race has priced one horse at roughly 12 times the other's exit.

## The Bet Is on AI, Not Plastic

Dragoneer and Thrive aren't paying 40x revenue for a corporate card. According to [The Next Web's summary of the Bloomberg report](https://thenextweb.com/news/ramp-60bn-valuation-1-85bn-round), Ramp has been pushing into AI since its June round: in August it released a tool that routes tasks to cheaper AI models where it can, and it is considering training its own models for the first time.

That routing tool, which PYMNTS identifies as Router.com, is the more interesting signal. Ramp says it used the system internally for three years and that it cut its AI costs by about 30%. It's the same economics that pushed investors into infrastructure plays like [Together AI's $800 million bet on cheaper open models](/article/together-ai-800m-series-c-open-source): most companies don't need the most expensive model for every task, they need the cheapest one that works. Ramp already sits on the spending data of 70,000 businesses. Selling them software that decides which model to pay for is a natural extension.

Read that way, the round is less a bet on a card issuer than on software that runs more of the finance department, from expense reports and payments to AI-powered fraud detection, with Ramp choosing the models underneath. Whether that justifies $60 billion depends on whether the AI products bring in revenue, not just headlines.

## What Could Make the Gap Close

There are real reasons for caution. The round isn't confirmed by Ramp, the revenue figure is four months old, and private valuations in 2026 have been set by a small group of crossover investors competing for the same few winners, the same dynamic behind the [trillion-dollar talk around OpenAI's IPO filing](/article/openai-ipo-trillion-dollar-filing). Those funds increasingly own competing bets, a strain that [even Benchmark now admits is breaking VC's rule against backing rivals](/article/benchmark-vc-no-rivals-rule-factory-cognition). A $60 billion mark is a promise about the future that Ramp now has to grow into.

Brex is also no longer a cash-strapped startup. It now has a large bank's balance sheet and customer base behind it. If Capital One can sell Brex's tools to its existing business customers, the competition Ramp faces gets harder, not easier.

For now, though, the scoreboard reads $60 billion against $5.15 billion. In corporate spend, at least, the market has picked a winner, and it picked very fast.
