*Correction (October 9, 2026): An earlier version of this article's headline said Qualcomm's "own CEO" killed the deal. The denial came from Jim Keller, the CEO of Tenstorrent, not from Qualcomm. The talks were reported, not confirmed, so we no longer describe them as "advanced" or say Qualcomm was "about to" buy the company. We also removed claims that Tenstorrent's valuation tripled on the rumor and that investors repriced the company, which we could not verify, and corrected the description of Intel's reported interest.*

For two weeks, the story wrote itself: Qualcomm, hunting for a way into the data-center AI market Nvidia has locked down, was in talks to acquire Tenstorrent, the RISC-V accelerator startup run by chip legend Jim Keller, at a price of $8 billion to $10 billion. [The Information broke it on June 15](https://sherwood.news/markets/qualcomm-reportedly-in-talks-to-acquire-ai-chip-design-company-tenstorrent/), citing a person with direct knowledge of the talks, and [Reuters](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/qualcomm-talks-acquire-ai-chip-230401789.html) and much of the tech press picked it up. Then, at the end of June, at a media event in Tokyo, Keller [said Tenstorrent has not been in acquisition talks with Qualcomm](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/qualcomms-tenstorrent-deal-looks-less-121327676.html). A deal that looked like one of the biggest AI hardware transactions of the summer was contradicted by the one person who would actually know.

## The Deal That Made Too Much Sense

The logic behind the rumored acquisition was never in question, which is exactly why it spread so fast. Qualcomm has been trying to move from being a smartphone-chip company into a data-center AI player, and Tenstorrent was the cleanest shortcut available: an open RISC-V instruction set instead of licensed ARM or x86, an AI accelerator architecture, and Keller himself, whose résumé (Apple, AMD, Tesla) is the closest thing the chip industry has to a guaranteed credibility stamp. A $10 billion price tag for that combination looks cheap relative to what Nvidia's market cap implies the alternative is worth. Most outlets that covered the story ran some version of the same framing: Qualcomm buying a seat at the AI silicon table it has been locked out of.

## Why the Denial Matters More Than the Rumor

Startup CEOs deny acquisition talks all the time without actually killing the talks — it's standard practice to preserve negotiating leverage or avoid spooking employees and customers mid-process. What makes Keller's denial notable is the venue. He didn't issue a one-line statement through a PR handler; he addressed it at a media event in Tokyo and said the company is focused on building its own business, including its IP operations and high-end scalable AI work in Japan and other markets — not on being acquired. He also said Tenstorrent is talking with many companies in strategic areas, and that interest in big deals across the sector has increased. That combination — a flat denial on the acquisition, openness to other kinds of deals — reads less like a negotiating tactic and more like a startup that wants to stay independent, at least for now.

## The Broader AI Silicon Land Grab Doesn't Slow Down

Whether or not this specific deal ever comes back, the pressure that produced it hasn't gone anywhere. [Bloomberg reported in May](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/mergers-and-acquisitions/ai-chip-startup-tenstorrent-is-said-to-draw-interest-from-rivals) that both Intel and Qualcomm had held conversations with Tenstorrent about a possible takeover, and that the company had been talking to investment banks about its options. The reason is simple: the alternative to buying an AI accelerator business is spending years building one from scratch while Nvidia's CUDA moat gets deeper. The speed with which The Information's report spread is itself evidence of how much appetite there is for a credible RISC-V challenger to Nvidia. If Keller's denial is genuine and durable, expect the acquisition interest to redirect toward Tenstorrent's competitors, or resurface with a different suitor and a cleaner story down the line.

## The Takeaway

This is what an AI hardware frenzy looks like in real time: a $10 billion number gets attached to a private company based on a single-sourced report, the market treats it as close to fact for two weeks, and then the target's own CEO contradicts it in public. Nothing about Tenstorrent's underlying technology or Qualcomm's strategic need changed between June 15 and the end of June — only the story did. The lesson for anyone tracking AI infrastructure M&A isn't that this deal failed; it's that in this market, a reported price can travel a long way before a single signature exists.
