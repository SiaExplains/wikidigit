NASA's Voyager 1 became the first spacecraft to reach one light-day from Earth at 5:16:07 a.m. EST on Wednesday, November 18, 2026, about 16.09 billion miles (25.9 billion km) out. A radio command now takes a full day to get there and the answer takes another day to come back. A month earlier the mission had a scare: on October 18 and 19, NASA's Deep Space Network couldn't hear the probe at all, and its signal only came back on October 20, roughly 48 hours after the outage began.

## Forty-Nine Years to Cover One Light-Day

Voyager 1 launched on September 5, 1977, and NASA says it took 49 years, two months and 11 days to reach this distance. JPL puts its speed at about 38,000 mph (61,155 kph). It flew past Jupiter in 1979 and Saturn in late 1980. In February 1998 it passed Pioneer 10 to become the most distant human-made object, and on August 25, 2012, it crossed the heliopause into interstellar space.

According to NASA, one light-day works out to 16,094,799,096 miles. EarthSky puts it at 173.14 times the Earth-Sun distance, and Science News says that is more than four times as far as Pluto is from the Sun. JPL says crossing it causes no physical change to the spacecraft. It only lengthens the wait on the ground.

Suzanne Dodd, the Voyager project manager at JPL, said what that looks like in practice: "you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response." The link itself is slow, too. BBC Sky at Night Magazine reports commands go up at 16 bits per second and science data comes down at about 160 bits per second.

## The Two Days Voyager 1 Went Quiet

JPL says engineers traced it to a set of thrusters that had stopped working. Voyager 1 switched to its backup thrusters on its own, and its fault protection system put it in a stable configuration and paused normal activity so controllers had time to investigate. NASA hasn't said what caused the failure.

"We're relieved to be back in contact with Voyager 1 and are eager to understand what happened," said Kareem Badaruddin, the Voyager mission manager at JPL. He also said the team is "at a point in the mission where we have to expect the unexpected."

The thrusters matter because they keep the antenna pointed at Earth. Sky at Night says those attitude thrusters will eventually stop working, and that by 2036 NASA's antennas won't be able to detect the probe's signal anyway.

## Running on Half Its Launch Power

Both Voyagers run on radioisotope thermoelectric generators, which turn heat from decaying plutonium into electricity. JPL says each probe loses about 4 watts of generating capacity a year, and Sky at Night says output is now around half of what it was at launch.

The team has been shutting things down to keep the rest alive. JPL turned off Voyager 1's Low-energy Charged Particles experiment on April 17, 2026, and says seven of the ten instruments on each spacecraft have been switched off so far. In early September, engineers ran what they called the "Big Bang" on Voyager 1, replacing three devices with lower-power ones in a single coordinated operation, about six weeks after doing the same on Voyager 2. The swaps had to happen at once because the devices help regulate internal temperature. Without the change, NASA could have had to shut off another instrument as early as summer 2027.

Science News reports that scientists expect the power to run out in 10 to 15 years, at which point the probes stop sending data. Voyager 1 also had a memory-chip failure in late 2023 that took NASA five months to diagnose, according to Sky at Night.

## Why Earth Keeps Catching Up With Voyager

The distance to Earth doesn't grow in a straight line. Earth orbits the Sun at about 30 km/s, faster than Voyager 1's roughly 17 km/s, so for part of each year our planet swings toward the probe faster than the probe moves away, EarthSky explains. Orbital Radar's tracker says the gap keeps growing until January 2027, shrinks slightly until April 2027, and stays about 27 million km beyond one light-day even at its lowest point.

The probe is still working in a region no other spacecraft measures directly. Princeton physicist Jamie Rankin told Science News, "Every day, it's in places nobody's ever explored before." Its twin, Voyager 2, is about 2 billion miles closer and should reach one light-day in November 2035.

Voyager's timescale is unusual even for space science.
