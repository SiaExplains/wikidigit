HD 158485, one of the stars astronomers use to calibrate the James Webb Space Telescope's brightness measurements, is actually two stars orbiting each other every 382.8 days. A team at the Xinjiang Astronomical Observatory of the Chinese Academy of Sciences found the companion in roughly 2,000 days of NASA TESS data, and their paper in The Astrophysical Journal Letters estimates that modeling the system as one star shifts its predicted infrared flux by about 1.6% to 2.5%. The paper came out on October 27, the Chinese Academy of Sciences announced it on November 23, and Universe Today covered it on November 26.

## Webb Needs Reference Stars It Can Trust

A telescope camera records counts. Turning those counts into a physical brightness means comparing them with stars whose spectra astronomers believe they can predict, and checking what the instrument sees against what the model says it should see.

HD 158485, also catalogued as HR 6514, is one of those stars. The paper describes it as an A4 V star in the CALSPEC network and the JWST absolute-flux calibration program, part of what the authors call the "JWST A-dwarf calibration network." Gaia measures it at magnitude 6.48. Until this study, its model treated it as a single star.

## Six Pulsations That Disagreed With Each Other

HD 158485 is a delta Scuti star, which means it pulsates on short, regular cycles. The team, led by Chenglong Lv, picked out six stable pulsation modes in TESS's 2-minute-cadence photometry from Sectors 14 to 26, 40 to 41, 47 to 60 and 73 to 86.

Those pulsations work like clocks. A star moving in an orbit is alternately a little closer to us and a little farther away, so its signals arrive slightly early and then slightly late. In HD 158485 the six modes fell into two groups whose timing drifted in opposite directions, which is what you'd expect if each group came from a different star. The paper's title states the result: TESS pulsation timing "reveals the JWST calibration star HD 158485 as a double-pulsator binary," a pair in which both stars pulsate.

From the timing the team measured an orbital period of 382.80 days and an eccentricity of 0.242, a moderately stretched orbit. The projected light-travel-time amplitudes are about 195.8 and 239.7 seconds for the two stars, and the companion has about 0.818 times the mass of the primary. Universe Today describes the companion as most likely an F-type main-sequence star.

## A Prediction Tested on Data It Hadn't Seen

Timing signals like these can come from noise or from slow drifts in a star's pulsations, so the authors ran a blind test. They fit the orbit using only the first two long TESS campaigns (Sectors 14 to 26 and 47 to 60), then used that model, unchanged, to predict the timing in the third campaign, Sectors 73 to 86.

The paper reports a correlation of 0.983 between predicted and observed timing, with a held-out scatter of 83.8 seconds. A model with the period fixed at 365.25 days left a scatter of 289.9 seconds, so the 383-day orbit fits the new data far better than a one-year cycle would. According to the CAS release, the earlier model predicted the later timing "with accuracy." The paper doesn't report spectroscopic follow-up, so for now the orbit rests on pulsation timing alone.

## What 1.6% Means for a Space Telescope

For Webb, what matters is how much a hidden companion changes the star's expected spectrum. The team built a two-atmosphere model of the system from the measured mass ratio and compared it with a conventional single-star model. The predicted flux differs by about 1.6% between 1 and 5 microns and by about 2.5% between 5 and 28 microns.

Universe Today points out that these figures are not a measured error in JWST's calibration. They show how far the expected brightness moves when a binary is modeled as one star, and they back the paper's argument that calibration standards should be checked for companions. Absolute-flux calibration feeds into every brightness measurement built on it, so an offset of a couple of percent in a reference star is worth knowing about. Precision results such as the [Hubble census that found dwarf galaxies kept just 11% of their oxygen](/article/hubble-dwarf-galaxies-missing-oxygen-11-percent) lean on flux measurements from several observatories in the same way.

## TESS Keeps Finding Things It Wasn't Built For

NASA built TESS to find planets by watching stars dim as something passes in front of them. In this case its long light curves timed a star's pulsations closely enough to weigh an unseen partner. The CAS release credits "long-term observations from NASA's TESS satellite," and the roughly 2,000-day baseline is what made a 383-day orbit measurable in the first place.

Timing light carefully has a way of paying off in astronomy. [Voyager 1 is now one light-day from Earth](/article/voyager-1-one-light-day-earth-silent-two-days), and every command sent to it has to account for that delay. For HD 158485, the open questions are whether spectra will confirm the two stars' motion and whether calibration teams will add a second star to its model.
