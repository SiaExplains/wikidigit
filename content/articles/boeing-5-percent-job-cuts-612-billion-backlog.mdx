Boeing is cutting about 5% of its commercial airplane workforce, roughly 2,500 jobs, because it is building jets more slowly than it planned. Stephanie Pope, who runs Boeing Commercial Airplanes, told employees in a [memo on November 9, 2026](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/boeing-to-cut-5-of-planemaking-divisions-workforce-seattle-times-reports-5012939) that slower production increases have left the division overstaffed. The same division ended September with a [record $612 billion backlog](https://boeing.mediaroom.com/2026-10-27-Boeing-Reports-Third-Quarter-Results) covering more than 6,300 airplanes. Airlines want the planes. Boeing has decided it needs fewer people to build them at the pace it can actually manage.

## What Pope's Memo Says

Pope's message gave no headcount, but it was blunt about the cause. "Adjusting our production schedules and reducing aircraft output puts us in an overstaffed position," she wrote, according to [Anadolu Agency](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/economy/boeing-to-cut-5-of-commercial-airplane-workforce-as-production-ramp-up-slows/4117929). "Unfortunately, that means we need to align our staffing levels to our revised business plan."

She also said Boeing needs "to reset the pace of our production rate ramps to provide our factories and supply chain partners additional time to achieve stability," and admitted that after two years of investing in people, training and infrastructure, "we have not yet fully recovered."

Boeing didn't give a headcount for the cut. Reuters reported that the commercial division had 50,494 employees as of December 2025, and [AeroTime](https://www.aerotime.aero/articles/boeing-commercial-airplanes-workforce-cuts-737-production) put the reduction at about 2,500 jobs on that basis. Some of it will come from attrition and the rest from involuntary layoffs. Notices could go out as early as December, and Reuters notes that many states require 60 days' warning. Boeing says affected employees will get outplacement services, layoff benefits and access to its Employee Assistance Program.

## The 737 and 787 Ramps Slipped

The memo follows a rough third quarter. Boeing began moving 737 output from 42 to 47 jets a month in the second quarter of 2026, with the FAA's approval, and had planned to reach 47 by summer. On the October earnings call, CEO Kelly Ortberg pushed that to early 2027 and said Boeing would miss its 2026 737 delivery goal. Executives said the 787 Dreamliner would miss its year-end delivery target too.

The 787 was meant to go from 8 jets a month to 10. It stayed at 8 through the third quarter, and Boeing's own results describe the program as "stable" at that rate. Reuters also reported last month that the 767 and 777 programs have been disrupted. The 777X is still due for first delivery in 2027.

In the quarter, the commercial unit delivered 161 airplanes, including 124 737s and 22 787s, according to AeroTime. It booked 300 net orders from airlines including Turkish Airlines, Ethiopian Airlines and Lufthansa. Revenue was $10.7 billion and the division lost $697 million.

## Orders Aren't the Problem

Boeing's total company backlog reached a record $745 billion at the end of September. The trouble is turning orders into delivered jets, since every month a ramp slips pushes revenue and cash further out. Boeing now doesn't expect free cash flow to reach $10 billion until 2029, a year later than analysts had expected, Reuters reported.

Boeing hired and trained for a factory running faster than it runs today. When the rate target moves out by six months or more, the people hired for that rate cost money before they can produce anything, and the cut brings payroll back in line with the slower plan.

The stock barely reacted. After Boeing delayed the 737 increase two weeks earlier, its shares fell to an 18-month low, Anadolu reported. On the day the memo was reported, Reuters said the stock was down less than 1% in afternoon trading.

## The First Cut Since the 17,000

[Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-09/boeing-cuts-5-of-commercial-workforce-amid-factory-ramp-delays) called this Boeing's first job reduction in nearly two years. The last one was Ortberg's plan to cut 17,000 jobs in 2024, announced shortly after he took over, when Boeing was dealing with a machinists' strike and the fallout from a door plug that blew out of an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9.

This round is smaller. In 2024 the cuts came under heavy financial pressure; this time the trigger is a factory recovery that is running behind Boeing's own plan. Reuters reported that many Boeing workers had already worried layoffs were coming.

The timing matches a year of big companies trimming staff for very different reasons. [Meta cut 8,000 jobs to fund AI spending](/article/meta-8000-layoffs-ai-capex), [Oracle told regulators AI drove 21,000 cuts](/article/oracle-21000-ai-layoffs-regulators) and [Netflix let about 1,000 people go](/article/netflix-1000-layoffs-bajaria-dan-lin-earnings) to get growth back. Boeing's reason is more old-fashioned. It has more orders than it can fill, and it can't build planes as fast as it said it would.

What to watch next is whether early 2027 holds for the 737. If Boeing reaches 47 a month on the new date, this cut will look like a one-time reset. If the date slips again, Pope's overstaffing argument could come back with it.
