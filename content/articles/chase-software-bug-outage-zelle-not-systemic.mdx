A software bug at JPMorgan Chase slowed transactions on Monday, November 16, 2026, and the knock-on delays reached customers at several other big U.S. banks and on Zelle. Chase blamed "a temporary issue related to a software bug in an on-premise hardware system," said it was not cyber-related, and insisted to [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/16/jpmorgan-chase-technical-issue.html) that "this is not a systemic issue." Thousands of people reported problems that afternoon at Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, U.S. Bank, Truist and Capital One, according to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/17/us-bank-outage-what-caused-chase-problems-and-zelle-payment-delays).

## From 11:30 a.m. to "Processing Normally"

CNBC says users reported errors as early as 11:30 a.m. Eastern. Reports on Downdetector peaked around 2 p.m. Eastern and fell off later in the day, according to AFP figures cited by Al Jazeera. Some Chase customers got a message reading: "Some services aren't available. We're working on them. Please try back later."

Chase told CNBC that some transactions were taking longer than usual and that customers could still get to their accounts and funds. Al Jazeera reported that some customers said they couldn't get into their accounts or funds. A spokesperson said the bank was "working diligently to resolve this by the end of the day today."

The bank roughly hit that target. According to NBC News, cited by Al Jazeera, Chase said transactions were posting normally by about 6:30 p.m. Eastern, and [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Business/us-banks-reporting-technical-issues-online-transactions/story?id=138187551) reported a Monday-night update saying transactions were "now processing normally." Normal processing doesn't mean every delayed payment had already landed, as Al Jazeera pointed out.

## Why Other Banks Showed Up on Outage Maps

Much of the trouble at other banks came from payments that had to move to or from Chase. Chase spokesman Joe Evangelisti told [PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/customers-at-major-banks-are-experiencing-issues-accessing-their-accounts) that other banks were affected only "to the extent that they were a party to a transaction with us." CNBC reported the same thing: outage trackers listed other big banks because transactions between Chase and those institutions seemed to be stuck.

Several of those banks gave the same account. U.S. Bank said its systems were "not directly impacted," but warned that "clients attempting to send or receive Zelle payments with another financial institution may experience transaction delays." Capital One and Truist told Al Jazeera their services were running normally. PBS reported that Bank of America and Citigroup weren't having problems with their own services. ABC noted that the Federal Reserve's systems, including ACH transfers between banks, were operating normally.

## Zelle Was Up. Payments Still Stalled.

Zelle is where most people felt it. Early Warning Services, which runs the network, said Zelle was fully operational, and a spokesman told PBS that "an issue at the financial institutions may be delaying Zelle payments for some of their customers." ABC quoted Zelle as saying the network "is not down" and was "operating as it should."

Zelle carries the payment instructions, but the banks on either end still have to post the money, so a slow bank at one end holds up the transfer. According to ABC, more than 2,400 financial institutions use Zelle. Early Warning itself is owned by Bank of America, Capital One, JPMorgan Chase, PNC, Truist, U.S. Bank and Wells Fargo, so Chase is one of the owners of the network its bug slowed down.

Chase's "not systemic" line depends on how you count. By Al Jazeera's tally, customers of at least five other banks had problems during an afternoon that ran from late morning to early evening, but the other banks' own systems stayed up, and that is the distinction Chase is drawing. For a customer whose transfer sat in limbo, that distinction may not count for much.

## What Chase Still Hasn't Said

Chase's explanation is short. It hasn't named the hardware system, said what the bug did, or said how many customers were affected. None of the outlets that covered the outage reported a figure beyond "thousands" of reports.

It also landed in a bad week for infrastructure. Microsoft had a widespread outage on Thursday, November 13, that hit Outlook. On Friday the FAA ordered a nationwide ground stop after a failure in air traffic control communications. United Airlines had an internal system outage on Monday, which it said was fixed. Al Jazeera notes that nothing links these incidents to the Chase bug, and PBS described the problem as "a Chase-only issue."

Jody Westby, CEO of Global Cyber Risk, told Al Jazeera that "I don't think that any of that is a complete explanation the public deserves," and called for more detailed investigations. The finance world has seen what happens when a firm loses control of its machines: in Japan, [GMO asked customers to unplug their PCs](/article/gmo-8-billion-yen-ransom-unplug-pcs) after refusing a ransom. Nothing like that happened here, and Chase has said there's no evidence of an attack. The episode does show how much of everyday banking runs through a handful of shared rails, at the same time [crypto firms like Ripple are getting federal bank charters](/article/ripple-national-trust-bank-occ-charter-icba-lawsuit) to plug into the same system.
