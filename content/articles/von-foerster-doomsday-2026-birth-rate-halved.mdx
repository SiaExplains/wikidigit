Today, Friday, November 13, 2026, is the day the human population becomes infinite, according to a paper the journal Science published on November 4, 1960. "Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026," by University of Illinois physicist Heinz von Foerster and his students Patricia M. Mora and Lawrence W. Amiot, fitted a curve to 2,000 years of population estimates and recommended cutting the global birth rate by half. There are about 8.3 billion people alive today, by the UN figures Popular Science cites, and the birth rate is now slightly less than half its 1960 level.

## How the Equation Lands on a Friday the 13th

The authors took 24 estimates of world population going back 2,000 years and looked for a formula that matched them. Ordinary exponential growth didn't fit, because the growth rate itself kept rising as the population got bigger. Their curve divides a constant of about 179 billion by the number of years left until 2026.87. As the year gets close to that value the denominator shrinks to zero and the population heads to infinity. The paper put the date at 2026.87, give or take about five years.

Their explanation for the speed-up was that people are unlike fruit flies, whose populations level off. Humans communicate and form what the paper called "coalitions," so a bigger population gets better at keeping itself alive, and that speeds growth further. The paper broke with Thomas Malthus on how it ends: "Our great-great-grandchildren will not starve," the authors wrote. "They will be squeezed to death."

The decimal 2026.87 falls in mid-November. November 13, 2026 is a Friday the 13th, and it was von Foerster's birthday. He was born in Vienna on November 13, 1911 and died in 2002, so today would have been his 115th.

## Critics Called It "Manifestly Absurd," Then Textbooks Cited It

Time and The New York Times covered the paper, the comic strip Pogo parodied it, and many commentators read it as satire, according to Alan MacEachern, a historian at Western University who traced its reception. Mathematicians and demographers wrote to Science calling the work "ridiculous" and "manifestly absurd." The authors answered the letters and defended their math.

After that the equation was taken more seriously than its title invited. MacEachern found it cited at face value in genetics, ecology and demography textbooks from the 1960s through the 1980s. A chemical-industry witness used it to argue for pesticides before a Canadian parliamentary committee in 1963, and it turned up in a 1971 U.S. Supreme Court abortion case.

It also held up against the data for a long time. Through the 1970s and 1980s its projections were more accurate than most demographers' forecasts, MacEachern writes. Around 1993, the actual population started falling behind the curve.

## The Growth Rate Peaked Just After Publication

"He got the picture right just as that picture ceased to be right," Joel Cohen, a mathematical biologist at Rockefeller University and Columbia University, told Popular Science. The population growth rate, Cohen said, hit its all-time peak in the early 1960s.

The world passed 8 billion people in 2022. Popular Science, citing Our World in Data, reports that around 55% of countries, home to more than two-thirds of the world's people, now have fertility below the replacement level of about 2.1 children per woman. Dean Spears, an economic demographer at the University of Texas, told the magazine the prediction was "based on a misunderstanding of where population growth comes from." Most of the growth, the article explains, came from more children surviving as sanitation, nutrition and germ theory spread, rather than from more births.

## The Birth Rate Halved, but Not the Way the Paper Wanted

Because people were unlikely to accept shorter lives, the authors proposed halving the global birth rate. Edinburgh epidemiologist Mark Woolhouse, writing in The Conversation this week, describes von Foerster's remedy as birth control "on a massive scale."

Governments tried versions of that. In the 1970s, international agencies pushed birth control aggressively and even made it a condition of food aid, Woolhouse writes. India ran forced sterilisation campaigns from 1975 to 1977, and China's one-child policy began in 1979 and lasted until 2015. Those programmes were abandoned. Spears argues birth rates would have fallen without them, and Cohen calls coercive birth policies "counterproductive."

Woolhouse's reading is that von Foerster missed the demographic transition, in which falling birth rates outweigh falling death rates as countries get richer. It was already under way in 1960. "Development was the answer," he writes. Japan, Hungary, Turkey and China now encourage couples to have more children.

## What a Failed Forecast Says About Today's

Woolhouse calls the paper a "cautionary tale" about worst-case modelling. A model's construction, he argues, predetermines what it can output, and von Foerster's equation was "predetermined to give the desired result." He applies the same test to current exercises that forecast catastrophic risks out to 2100, a list that includes malicious use of AI.

That applies to a lot of what we cover. Companies now raise money on curves that only go up, including the [$650M bet that AI can keep improving itself](/article/recursive-superintelligence-650m-self-improving-ai), and the checking usually comes later, as it did with the [AI-found 3D 'Einstein' tile that mathematicians are still vetting](/article/ai-chair44-3d-einstein-tile-monotile). The same tools are already inside research, where an NBER study found [AI writing in 29% of new US STEM dissertations](/article/ai-29-percent-us-stem-phd-dissertations).

MacEachern, who followed the paper from its Time coverage to its textbook citations, takes a narrower lesson from it. "For me, the lesson is in how we present our beliefs to the world," he writes.
